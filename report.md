# Collocation Analysis of "junzi" in *The Analects* and *The I Ching*

In ancient China, the term “junzi” was the highest praise for a person. From emperors to ordinary people, no matter what school of thought they followed, anyone called junzi was recognized for good character and behaviour. Even so, there was no single standard. Different people and schools held different ideas about what made a junzi. This study uses corpus collocation analysis to explore the meaning of junzi in *The Analects* and *The I Ching*. It changes the range of nearby words repeatedly to observe how collocation results shift. The research compares p-values and LogDice scores, and carries out close reading using KWIC lines. This manual check tells apart real semantic links and false co-occurrences caused by fixed sentence patterns in ancient texts. It then identifies differences between the two books in their description of junzi.

Confucianism held a central place in ancient Chinese rule. Its classic texts are widely accepted and suitable for research. Both *The Analects* and *The I Ching* belong to the core works of the Four Books and Five Classics, but they were written in different times and focus on different ideas. This makes them good for comparison. The digital texts used in this study come from Project Gutenberg. Only the main body of the original books is kept. Translators’ prefaces, footnotes, comments and appendices are removed to keep the original vocabulary features of pre-Qin texts.

After text preprocessing, two measures are used: p-value and LogDice. The p-value tests whether two words appear together by chance. A smaller p-value means the chance of random co-occurrence is lower, and the result is more statistically significant. LogDice measures how strongly words form stable collocations. Higher LogDice scores mean two words often appear together as a set phrase. Since the two measures work in different ways, they produce different collocation rankings. Once collocation lists are generated, KWIC is used to pull original sentences with frequent word pairs for manual reading. This step distinguishes two cases: real semantic connections between words, and false co-occurrences created by fixed ancient sentence structures. Raw numbers cannot directly show the writers’ ideas. All selected sentences must be checked within their original context.

The results show that LogDice and p-value rankings do not match. LogDice is a reliable measure for corpus collocation studies. It works well for comparing co-occurrence strength across different texts. It balances the frequency of each word and avoids overstating rare random pairs, a problem seen in some other association measures. The p-value focuses on checking whether word pairs appear together by accident. The two methods do not produce identical rankings. Some words have significant p-values but low LogDice scores. This means they appear often across the whole text, yet do not have a stable link with junzi. Other words get high LogDice ranks but low total frequency and weak significance. These pairs are rare, but closely linked when they do appear. Take results from *The I Ching* as an example: “xiaoren” has a very low p-value and high LogDice score, forming a strong fixed collocation. Other words with significant p-values and low LogDice are just common words in the text and do not form special collocations with junzi. Relying on only one measure will lead to bias, so manual line-by-line checks with KWIC are necessary.

Manual reading of KWIC lines from *The Analects* and *The I Ching* shows clear differences. In *The Analects*, junzi often appears with words related to moral judgement. Famous lines include “The junzi understands what is right; the xiaoren understands what is profitable”, “The junzi seeks harmony but not conformity”, “The junzi unites people, not cliques” and “The junzi is calm and open”. Related words focus on righteousness, xiaoren, harmony, unity and inner peace. They talk about personal virtue, social behaviour and moral boundaries. *The Analects* regularly puts junzi beside xiaoren to contrast two levels of moral character, focusing on self-cultivation and human relations. In *The I Ching*, words paired with junzi are about timing, action and changes in nature. Key terms include constant self-improvement, all days, the Qian trigram, advance and retreat, good conduct, hidden talent, and rise and fall. Representative lines are “The junzi keeps constant self-improvement” and “The junzi hides his talent and waits for the right moment to act”. Here, junzi observes natural trends, follows natural rules and changes actions according to good or bad situations. Though *The I Ching* also contrasts junzi with xiaoren, the comparison focuses on timing and change, not simply good and bad morality. In short, *The Analects* describes junzi as a moral role model. Its related words centre on self-cultivation and upholding moral principles. *The I Ching* describes junzi as a careful decision-maker who reads situations, knows when to move or wait, hides ability and keeps working through change. Both texts use xiaoren as a negative example to support their values.

This study has several limits. First, the corpus only contains *The Analects* and *The I Ching*, so the dataset is small. The digital texts have not been checked against different old versions or unearthed manuscripts. The source materials are limited and can be expanded in future work. Second, horizon only measures word position and does not equal semantic connection. No matter what horizon value we choose, the algorithm only counts character distance and cannot recognise sentence structure or text logic. Statistical measures such as LogDice only show co-occurrence strength and cannot fully represent semantic collocation. Therefore, manual KWIC reading is essential.
